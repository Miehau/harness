## Two-proposal comparison — not a three-candidate synthesis

The fixture establishes the migration problem: `enqueue` returns a numeric ID synchronously, while `worker.js` removes a job before awaiting a payload-only delivery callback. A rejection loses that job; the queue and its IDs are in memory (`queue.js:1-5`, `worker.js:1-5`, `test.mjs:1-4`). `docs/decision.md:1-3` documents that loss as acceptable for the former prototype, not as a durability guarantee. The sole relevant commit is `b731c08`.

| Rubric criterion | Proposal A (Terra) | Proposal B (Sol) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Recovery/delivery — A: 3/5; B: 4/5** | A’s **“Delivery, acknowledgement, and recovery”** keeps the row until committed ack and correctly identifies the post-external-success duplicate window. But it commits an attempt *before* invoking the callback: repeated crashes before callback invocation can exhaust `maxAttempts` and dead-letter a job never delivered. | B’s **“Shape”** and crash matrix leave an in-flight row pending until ack, including across callback failure and restart. Backoff permits later eligible jobs to overtake it. Its one-consumer premise needs enforcement beyond the proposed in-process opener guard: a second process could otherwise run an external callback concurrently. |
| **2. Compatibility — A: 4/5; B: 4/5** | A’s **“Usage”** preserves synchronous numeric `enqueue`, including the observed first-ID assertion, and explicitly migrates `new Queue`, free `drain`, and destructive `take`. | B’s **“Usage”** makes the same observed-caller migration, preserves payload as the callback’s first argument, and distinguishes one-pass `drain` from ongoing `serve`. Both proposals narrow previously unrestricted payloads to JSON; the fixture establishes only string payload use, not compatibility for other callers. |
| **3. Operational simplicity — A: 3/5; B: 3/5** | A’s **“Persistent state and ownership”** offers a local store, explicit close, dead letters and a lifetime exclusive lock, but delegates retry timing/startup looping to the application. Its proposed built-in SQLite engine floor and lifetime-lock mechanism need deployment verification. | B’s **“Shape”** includes restart serving and scheduled retry without an application retry loop. The native binding, timers, WAL backup procedure, and accumulating pending/quarantined rows add operational duties; its in-process ownership guard does not establish single-process ownership. Neither needs a broker. |
| **4. Evidence-grounded tradeoffs — A: 3/5; B: 4/5** | A’s **“Problem”** correctly separates the documented prototype rationale from the new requirement, but its asserted configured Node 22.19 runtime is not established by the supplied fixture. Its **“Ordering”** says redrive retains an ID yet moves a job to the tail, while its **“Persistent state”** says ready order is ascending ID; that requires another ordering field or a changed rule. | B’s **“Problem”** likewise distinguishes source facts from inference. Its **“Open questions”** correctly notes that numeric IDs can collide after database restoration or across databases. A’s proposed `local-queue:${id}` receiver key lacks that distinction. |
| **5. Testability — A: 4/5; B: 4/5** | A’s **“Deterministic verification plan”** specifies reopen, failure, commit-fault and child-kill checks. Tests should additionally cover crash-before-callback attempt exhaustion and redrive order. | B’s **“Next implementation step”** specifies temporary files, fake time, fault injection and a direct reopen smoke. It also needs a two-process ownership test; the stated in-process guard alone cannot prove that invariant. |

**Decision for owner discussion, not selection:** Use B’s pending-until-ack and explicit retry/serve semantics as the comparison baseline, and require A’s stronger lifetime ownership goal to be resolved before adopting either design. Choose explicitly whether failed heads block later jobs (A) or back off while later jobs proceed (B), and whether poison jobs enter a finite-attempt dead-letter state (A) or remain pending until operator quarantine (B). A’s finite limit needs protection against counting crashes before callback as exhausted deliveries; B’s quarantine has no same-ID redrive path. Both retain a synchronous API by paying synchronous local-commit latency, subject to a durable filesystem. Neither can prevent duplicate external effects without receiver-side, atomic idempotency keyed to a stable queue identity and job ID.

One additional acceptance boundary belongs in either design: a commit can succeed immediately before a crash that prevents the caller receiving the ID. On restart the job exists, although the producer does not know its ID. Both proposals explain pre-commit and post-commit delivery recovery, but neither specifies how a producer retry distinguishes that ambiguous acceptance.
